The RDM Life Cycle and FAIR

The Research Data Life Cycle

The Research Data Management (RDM) life cycle describes how research data evolves from its initial creation to its long‑term preservation and potential reuse. It emphasises that data is not static: it moves through stages of increasing stability, documentation, and governance. Although terminology varies across models, four stages are widely recognised.

Process

Data is created, cleaned, and transformed. Files are volatile and frequently modified, often stored on local machines or compute systems. Organisation is pragmatic and workflow‑driven.

Analyse

Data begins to stabilise. Cleaned datasets, derived outputs, and refined scripts emerge. Some files gain scientific identity and become candidates for long‑term retention.

Archived

Selected files are packaged with metadata and persistent identifiers to form digital objects. These are stored in preservation‑oriented environments and protected through integrity checks, replication, and retention policies.

Published

A subset of digital objects is curated for dissemination. They receive rich descriptive metadata, globally resolvable identifiers, and are made discoverable for external reuse.

The FAIR Data Principles

The FAIR Principles — Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable — provide a framework for making data usable by humans and machines.

Findable: Data is described with rich metadata and assigned persistent identifiers.

Accessible: Data and metadata are retrievable through standard protocols; access conditions are documented.

Interoperable: Data uses well‑defined formats, vocabularies, and standards.

Reusable: Data is richly described, licensed, and documented, with clear provenance and quality information.

Connecting FAIR and the RDM Life Cycle

The RDM Life Cycle describes how data evolves, while the FAIR” principles describe the qualities data must exhibit to remain usable by humans and machines. These two perspectives are complementary: one focuses on change, the other on usability.

A key insight is that FAIR does not “switch on” only at publication. Its scope shifts as data matures. Initially, FAIR supports collaboration and internal organisation. Later, it supports stewardship, preservation, and ultimately global reuse.

FAIR applies at every stage — the audience and purpose simply changes.

To understand how FAIR maps onto the life cycle, and what this implies for infrastructure and services, we need an intermediate layer: data states. Data states translate conceptual life‑cycle stages into operational conditions that storage systems, metadata services, and governance workflows can act upon.

We can distinguish five operational data states:

  1. Active — data is being created and modified; FAIR supports internal organisation and team‑level reuse.
  2. Closed — data stabilises; FAIR begins to support institutional stewardship and future interpretation.
  3. Locked — data is inactive and protected against accidental change; FAIR ensures it remains intelligible and manageable beyond the project, although responsibility has not yet transferred to a data steward.
  4. Archived & Preserved — data becomes a curated digital object, the responsibility is transferred to a data steward; FAIR enables long‑term discovery, integrity, and interoperability.
  5. Published — selected objects enter the scholarly record; FAIR supports global findability, accessibility, and reuse.

These states define how data is stored, who is responsible for it, and how richly it must be described.

In the early stages, FAIR is inward‑facing and pragmatic — supporting the Process and Analyse phases and the Active state, where data and metadata change frequently.

In mid‑life, FAIR supports the transition from volatile data to stewardship‑oriented processes. Researchers stabilise selected outputs to share with peers. This corresponds to the Closed and Locked states, where data is protected but not yet ready for archiving or publication.

At the end of the life cycle, FAIR becomes outward‑facing. In the Archived and Published states, FAIR enables institutional and global discovery, integrity, and reuse.

The five states tie the life cycle and FAIR together: each life‑cycle stage corresponds to a data state, and each data state demands a different expression of FAIR.

  • the life cycle describes how data changes,
  • the data states describe how data is managed,
  • and FAIR describes the qualities data should exhibit within each state.

In this way, the data states form the conceptual bridge that connects the evolving states of data (the life cycle) with the expectations placed on it (FAIR).

This mapping makes clear that data states are not an alternative to the life cycle but the mechanism through which the life cycle becomes actionable. It shows how conceptual stages translate into concrete metadata, services, and storage systems.

FAIR Across the RDM Life Cycle

Putting it all together

The following sections describe how FAIR manifests in each data state and how this aligns with the stages of the RDM Life Cycle.

FAIR for Teams

In the Active state, rapid iteration and collaboration dominate. Files change frequently, so the priority is capturing provenance — records of how data was collected, processed, and transformed. At this point, provenance is often the only reliable account of what happened, when, and why.

FAIR here is practical and inward‑facing: clear naming, shared access points, open formats, and well‑maintained notebooks that support reproducible teamwork.

  • Typical services: HPC scratch, project storage, Elab Journals, version control.

  • Audience: research team

  • Focus: efficient collaboration and early capture of provenance

FAIR for Stewardship (Locked)

During the Closed stage, data becomes sufficiently stable for structured organisation and description, but it is not yet ready for full stewardship. FAIR begins to support both sides: researchers consolidate provenance, and stewards gain the context needed for future decisions.

The Locked state then acts as a buffer. Stabilised data is frozen in a read‑only condition, protected against accidental changes while retention and appraisal take place. FAIR remains minimal but essential, ensuring the data stays intelligible and manageable even in inactivity.

  • Typical services: Tape Library, Cold Storage, Retention Systems, Workflow Engines, Metadata Templates.

  • Audience: research team + institutional stewards

  • Focus: stabilising data and consolidating provenance, safeguarding inactive data

FAIR for Stewardship (Archived)

At this stage, data becomes a managed digital object. Files are packaged with structured metadata, assigned persistent identifiers, and placed in systems designed for long‑term integrity and accessibility. Provenance gathered earlier provides the context needed for authenticity, appraisal, and future reuse.

FAIR now operates at the level of institutional governance: persistent identifiers, controlled access, preservation metadata, and versioning.

  • Typical services: institutional repositories, preservation systems.

  • Audience: institutional stewards and selected collaborators

  • Focus: long‑term integrity and contextual understanding

FAIR for Reuse

Selected digital objects are curated for dissemination. They receive rich descriptive metadata, globally resolvable identifiers, and are indexed in discovery portals. Provenance becomes critical for external users assessing quality and fitness for reuse.

Here FAIR reaches full scope: discoverability, standardised access, community vocabularies, licensing, and documentation that supports meaningful reuse.

  • Typical services: public repositories, DOI registration services, discovery portals.

  • Audience: global research community

  • Focus: discovery, citation, and reuse

Summary

This chapter began by presenting the RDM life cycle and FAIR as separate frameworks. By following data through its states, we can now see how they converge into a single, coherent model of stewardship.

  • The RDM life cycle describes how data evolves from volatile working files to curated, citable outputs.

  • The data states determine which storage, tools, and governance mechanisms apply at each stage.

  • The FAIR principles describe how data should behave, with their emphasis shifting as data matures:

    • FAIR for Teams in the Active and Closed states
    • FAIR for Stewardship in the Locked and Archived states
    • FAIR for Reuse in the Published state

Together, these elements form a unified framework for managing data from creation to long‑term reuse.

This alignment reveals that what moves through these states is not merely a collection of files, but a structured digital object. As data progresses through the life cycle, it becomes clear that files alone are insufficient. A file without context is difficult to interpret, impossible to trust, and often unusable. To be managed, preserved, and reused, data needs:

  • metadata describing what it is,
  • provenance explaining how it came to be,
  • identifiers enabling reference,
  • relationships linking it to other data, code, and publications.

In other words, data must be packaged and understood as a digital object, not just a set of files. This shift is essential for FAIR stewardship and long‑term preservation.

With the life cycle, FAIR, and data states now integrated, the digital object emerges as the natural foundation for modern research data management: the primary entity that infrastructures recognise, preserve, and serve.

The next chapter, The Digital Object, explores this concept in depth: what digital objects are, how they are constructed, and why they function as the core entities that research data infrastructures must support.
